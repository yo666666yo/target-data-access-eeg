Cross-subject EEG decoders are compared across papers whose evaluation protocols use the target subject's data differently. Subject-independent networks use none of it \cite{kwon2020subject}; Euclidean Alignment and the Riemannian transforms of the same family use the unlabelled signals to align feature distributions \cite{he2020transfer,zanini2018transfer,rodrigues2019riemannian}; calibration-block fine-tuning consumes a labelled block outright \cite{wu2022transfer}. The labels attached to these protocols (zero-calibration, unsupervised transfer, subject-adaptive) are coarse, and the accuracy differences between them are routinely read as the value of the extra access.

Partitioning and reporting choices are known to move BCI comparisons \cite{lotte2018review,roy2019deep}, and MOABB \cite{jayaram2018moabb,chevallier2024moabb} standardizes the interface but not the accounting: two pipelines can share a loader and a split while giving the target subject different influence over training, normalization and checkpoint choice. Alignment is filed under zero-calibration even though it reads every one of the evaluated subject's trials, so a protocol name records only what a method may label, leaving what it may see unstated. Compact convolutional references \cite{lawhern2018eegnet,schirrmeister2017deep} are evaluated under all of these protocols. Leakage drives irreproducible findings \cite{kapoor2023leakage,bouthillier2021variance} and small-sample neuroimaging yields error bars wider than reported \cite{varoquaux2018crossval,vabalas2019sample,poldrack2020best}, but that work is diagnostic, whereas choosing between protocols also requires measuring what a procedure is worth once everything else is matched.

Matching everything else is what published comparisons rarely can do. Adding a calibration block lengthens an epoch, so the adapted model also gets more optimizer steps; the target's share of a batch is whatever the cohort size implies; and if the block supplies the early-stopping signal, model selection has changed too. None of this is visible in a results table (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}a), and each can change the effect being claimed. Our own earlier work tracked those quantities rather than the procedure; Section~\ref{sec:controls} reports what replaying it reveals.

This paper narrows the problem to something that can be measured cleanly. On a fixed target test set, we measure what each way of using the target subject's available trials buys, once the source partition, the per-epoch sample budget, the early-stopping rule and the target sampling rate are held constant. Each held-out subject's trials are divided once into an available half and a reserved half on which every condition is scored (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}b). The available half is used in one of four ways: not at all, by Euclidean Alignment, by supervised training, or by both. This crosses two procedures that overlap in what they consume: supervised training reads the available trials' signals as well as their labels. The grid therefore measures what each procedure adds on top of the other, and leaves the value of signals against labels open.

The same accounting issue arises when the result is summarized. A cross-subject paper reports a spread as well as a mean, and the spread is read as between-subject heterogeneity. That holds only if the aggregation unit is the subject. We test it with a control that retrains nothing: the same per-trial predictions are aggregated by true subject and by random groups of the same sizes, so any difference is attributable to the grouping alone.

We contribute a controlled $2\times2$ of alignment against target supervision on three motor-imagery datasets, its controls checked against the realized indices; a fixed-prediction regrouping benchmark, with a matching variance estimate, for the aggregation unit's share of a between-subject spread; and an audit of the same comparison run without these controls. The procedures are standard; the controls, and an account of which of them cannot hold together, are new.
